\begin{abstract}
Sarcasm detection remains a challenging task in Natural Language Processing (NLP) due to the inherent complexity of figurative language where literal meaning often contradicts the intended sentiment. This paper presents a baseline approach for detecting sarcasm in news headlines using custom Word2Vec embeddings trained from scratch and a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network. We implement Word2Vec using Skip-gram architecture with negative sampling, deliberately avoiding pre-built libraries like Gensim to demonstrate fundamental understanding. Our model achieves 77.50\% accuracy on a dataset of 4,000 news headlines, with an impressive recall of 89.43\% and F1-score of 77.83\%, demonstrating strong effectiveness in identifying sarcastic content. We analyze the impact of various hyperparameters and demonstrate that increased dataset size and optimized training parameters significantly improve performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sarcasm is a form of figurative language where speakers convey meanings opposite to their literal words, often to express contempt, ridicule, or humor. This linguistic phenomenon poses significant challenges for automated sentiment analysis systems, as traditional approaches often misclassify sarcastic statements by analyzing only their surface-level sentiment. For instance, the phrase ``Oh great, another rainy day'' appears positive when analyzed literally but actually conveys negative sentiment.

The ability to detect sarcasm is crucial for various NLP applications, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sentiment Analysis}: Preventing misclassification of user opinions in product reviews and social media
    \item \textbf{Customer Feedback Analysis}: Accurately understanding customer satisfaction
    \item \textbf{Social Media Monitoring}: Identifying genuine praise versus sarcastic criticism
\end{itemize}

This project aims to build a robust sarcasm detection system using deep learning techniques. We focus on developing a baseline model that combines custom word embeddings with recurrent neural networks, specifically avoiding external embedding libraries to demonstrate complete implementation from fundamental principles.

\section{Problem Definition}

\subsection{Task Formulation}
Given a news headline $h = \{w_1, w_2, ..., w_n\}$ consisting of $n$ words, our task is to classify whether the headline is sarcastic or not. Formally, we define:

\begin{equation}
f(h) = \begin{cases} 
1 & \text{if } h \text{ is sarcastic} \\
0 & \text{if } h \text{ is not sarcastic}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $f: \mathcal{H} \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$ is our classification function mapping headlines to binary labels.

\subsection{Challenges}
Sarcasm detection presents several unique challenges:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Context Dependency}: Sarcasm often relies on shared knowledge or situational context not present in the text
    \item \textbf{Semantic Incongruity}: The literal meaning contradicts the intended meaning
    \item \textbf{Linguistic Markers}: Subtle cues like exaggeration, irony, or specific word choices
    \item \textbf{Domain Variability}: Sarcastic expressions vary across different contexts and cultures
\end{enumerate}

\section{Baseline Approach}

\subsection{Model Architecture}
Our baseline model consists of two main components:

\subsubsection{Word2Vec Embeddings}
We implemented Word2Vec from scratch using the Skip-gram architecture with negative sampling. Skip-gram predicts context words given a center word, learning distributed representations that capture semantic relationships.

\noindent For each word pair $(w_t, w_c)$ where $w_t$ is the target word and $w_c$ is a context word, we maximize:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \log \sigma(v_{w_c}^T v_{w_t}) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb{E}_{w_i \sim P_n(w)} [\log \sigma(-v_{w_i}^T v_{w_t})]
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, $k$ is the number of negative samples (10 in our case), and $P_n(w) \propto f(w)^{0.75}$ is the noise distribution based on word frequency. The first term maximizes the similarity between the target word and actual context words, while the second term minimizes similarity with randomly sampled words.

\subsubsection{Bidirectional LSTM Network}
We employed a Bidirectional LSTM to capture sequential dependencies in both forward and backward directions. The architecture consists of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Embedding Layer}: Maps words to 128-dimensional vectors
    \item \textbf{BiLSTM Layers}: 2 layers with 64 hidden units each
    \item \textbf{Pooling Layer}: Combines mean and max pooling for robust representation
    \item \textbf{Dense Layers}: Fully connected layers (256$\rightarrow$128$\rightarrow$32$\rightarrow$1) with batch normalization
    \item \textbf{Output Layer}: Sigmoid activation for binary classification
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Justification}

\subsubsection{Skip-gram with Negative Sampling}
We chose Skip-gram over alternative approaches such as CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words) for several reasons particularly suited to sarcasm detection. First, sarcastic headlines often employ unique or uncommon word combinations, and Skip-gram is better suited for rare words as it treats each context position as a separate training observation, generating more training examples per word compared to CBOW. Second, sarcasm detection relies heavily on capturing subtle word relationships and nuanced meanings; Skip-gram architecture learns richer, more detailed embeddings by predicting multiple context words from each center word, enabling the model to better understand contextual variations. Finally, Skip-gram generally achieves higher quality embeddings when working with limited data, making it ideal for our 4,000-sample training set.

\noindent Since traditional Word2Vec uses softmax over the entire vocabulary, which requires expensive computation of 
\begin{equation}
   P(w_{context}|w_{center}) = \exp(v_{w_c}^T v_{w_t})/\sum_{w \in V} \exp(v_w^T v_{w_t})
\end{equation}
 where the denominator sums over all vocabulary words ($V = 902$ in our case), making each training step computationally expensive. Negative sampling addresses this by transforming the problem: instead of predicting the correct context word from all possible words, we distinguish the actual context word (positive sample) from $k$ randomly selected words (negative samples). This reduces computational complexity from $O(|V|)$ to $O(k)$, where $k=10$ in our implementation, meaning only 11 words are updated per training iteration instead of the entire vocabulary. This custom Word2Vec implementation demonstrates understanding of embedding techniques and allows fine-tuning for domain-specific vocabulary in news headlines.

\subsubsection{Bidirectional LSTM}
Our choice of BiLSTM is motivated by several factors particularly suited to sarcasm detection. The bidirectional processing is crucial as it allows the model to understand context from both directions simultaneously. For instance, consider the headline "Oh great, another delay": processing forward captures the "Oh great" context while processing backward understands what comes after, and together the model comprehends that "Oh great, another delay" is sarcastic. This dual-direction processing is essential because sarcasm often depends on understanding both what precedes and what follows particular words.

\noindent Our architecture employs a dual pooling strategy that combines mean and max pooling to create a more robust sequence representation. Mean pooling computes the average representation across all words, capturing the overall tone of the headline, while max pooling identifies the most salient features by taking maximum values, effectively capturing the strongest sarcastic indicator words. Using both pooling types together provides a more comprehensive representation than relying on only the final LSTM output, as it combines both the general sentiment (mean) and peak sarcastic signals (max). Additionally, LSTM's gating mechanism captures long-range dependencies crucial for understanding contextual relationships, and BiLSTM has demonstrated strong performance in similar sentiment analysis tasks.

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}
We apply the following preprocessing steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lowercasing}: Convert all text to lowercase for normalization
    \item \textbf{Special Character Removal}: Remove punctuation and special characters, retaining only alphanumeric characters
    \item \textbf{Tokenization}: Split headlines into word tokens using whitespace
    \item \textbf{Vocabulary Building}: Construct vocabulary from training set with minimum word frequency of 2
    \item \textbf{Sequence Padding}: Pad/truncate sequences to uniform length of 50 tokens
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Split Strategy}
We employed stratified splitting to maintain class distribution:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training: 2,800 samples ($\sim$42\% sarcastic)
    \item Testing: 1,200 samples (validation during training)

\subsubsection{Word2Vec Implementation Details}

Our Word2Vec implementation follows the Skip-gram architecture with negative sampling. For each training step, we sample a center word from the corpus and predict its surrounding context words within the specified window size. The core training mechanism operates by taking a center word embedding and computing its similarity with a positive context word (actual neighbor), maximizing their dot product through the sigmoid function. Simultaneously, we sample $k=10$ negative words from the vocabulary using a unigram distribution raised to the power of 0.75, which provides a balanced sampling favoring frequent words while still including rare ones. For these negative samples, we minimize their similarity with the center word by maximizing the sigmoid of the negative dot product. The loss function combines both positive and negative components, and gradients are computed to update only the embeddings of the center word, the positive context word, and the $k$ negative samples. This approach is significantly more efficient than traditional softmax, as we update only $k+1$ embeddings per step rather than the entire vocabulary of 1,847 words. The embeddings are randomly initialized and iteratively refined through gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.025.

\subsubsection{BiLSTM Implementation Details}

Our BiLSTM model processes input sequences through several stages. The input consists of tokenized headlines represented as sequences of word indices (padded to length 50). These indices are first passed through an embedding layer that maps each word to its corresponding 128-dimensional Word2Vec representation learned in the previous stage. We apply embedding dropout (0.1) to prevent overfitting to specific word embeddings. The embedded sequence is then fed into a bidirectional LSTM layer with 64 hidden units per direction, resulting in 128-dimensional output vectors for each time step. The LSTM processes the sequence both forward (left-to-right) and backward (right-to-left), with each direction maintaining separate hidden and cell states. This produces contextualized representations where each word's encoding captures information from both its preceding and following context.

To aggregate the sequential LSTM outputs into a fixed-size representation, we employ a dual pooling strategy. Mean pooling computes the element-wise average across all time steps, capturing the overall semantic content and tone of the entire headline. Max pooling takes the element-wise maximum, identifying the strongest activations that often correspond to key sarcastic indicators or sentiment-bearing words. These two 128-dimensional pooled vectors are concatenated into a 256-dimensional feature vector that combines both comprehensive coverage and salient features.

The concatenated features pass through a three-layer feed-forward network with progressive dimensionality reduction: 256→128→32→1. The first dense layer is followed by batch normalization to stabilize training and improve convergence. ReLU activation functions introduce non-linearity after each hidden layer, and dropout (0.3) is applied between layers to prevent overfitting. The final layer outputs a single logit value, which represents the model's raw prediction score. During training, this logit is passed to BCEWithLogitsLoss which internally applies sigmoid and computes binary cross-entropy. During inference, we explicitly apply sigmoid to convert the logit to a probability between 0 and 1, where values above 0.5 indicate sarcasm.


\subsubsection{Key Findings from Hyperparameter Tuning}

Our systematic experiments revealed the following critical insights:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Word2Vec Learning Rate Impact:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reducing learning rate: 0.020 → 0.010
        \item Accuracy improvement: 66.00\% → 67.33\% (+1.33\%)
        \item Recall boost: 73.23\% → 82.68\% (+9.45\%)
        \item Explanation: Lower learning rate enables more stable gradient descent convergence, preventing oscillations around optimal embedding values and allowing finer adjustments to capture subtle semantic relationships in sarcastic language
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Word2Vec Training Epochs:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 10 → 20 epochs: Accuracy jumped 67.33\% → 73.50\% (+6.17\%), F1-score: 0.6818 → 0.7196
        \item 20 → 25 epochs: Accuracy increased to 75.83\% (+2.33\%)
        \item Explanation: Extended training allows embeddings to converge to more discriminative representations, capturing nuanced contextual patterns essential for distinguishing genuine from sarcastic statements
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Batch Size Scaling:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Batch 32 → 40: Accuracy 73.50\% → 75.83\% (+2.33\%)
        \item Batch 40 → 100: Accuracy 75.83\% → 77.50\% (+1.67\%)
        \item Explanation: Larger batches provide more stable gradient estimates by averaging over more samples, reducing variance in parameter updates and improving generalization. However, returns diminish at very large batch sizes due to reduced gradient noise beneficial for escaping local minima
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Dataset Size Effect:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Expansion: 1,000 samples → 4,000 samples (4× increase)
        \item Result: Significant performance gains across all metrics
        \item Explanation: Larger training corpus exposes both Word2Vec and BiLSTM to greater diversity of sarcastic patterns, idiomatic expressions, and contextual cues, enabling more robust feature learning
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Precision-Recall Trade-off Analysis:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Final model: Precision=68.90\%, Recall=89.43\% (recall-optimized)
        \item Config 3: Precision=70.13\%, Recall=78.87\% (more balanced)
        \item Trade-off: Final model sacrifices 1.23\% precision for 10.56\% recall gain
        \item Implication: Model favors identifying more sarcastic content at cost of some false positives, suitable for applications where missing sarcasm is more costly than occasional misclassification
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The final configuration (Batch=100, W2V Epochs=25, LR=0.010, 4,000 samples) represents the optimal balance, achieving 77.50\% accuracy and 0.7783 F1-score through synergistic effects of improved embeddings, stable training dynamics, and sufficient data diversity.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We employ standard classification metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: $\frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$
    \item \textbf{Precision}: $\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$
    \item \textbf{Recall}: $\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$
    \item \textbf{F1-Score}: $2 \times \frac{Precision \times Recall}{Precision + Recall}$
\end{itemize}

where TP, TN, FP, and FN represent true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives respectively.

\section{Analysis and Insights}

\subsection{Performance Analysis}

Our baseline model achieves 77.50\% accuracy with 77.83\% F1-score, demonstrating robust classification capability. The most notable strength is exceptionally high recall (89.43\%), making it effective for applications where missing sarcasm is costly. The BiLSTM architecture successfully captures bidirectional context, and the model shows good scalability, improving from 66\% (1K samples) to 77.50\% (4K samples). However, several limitations remain. Precision is moderate (68.90\%), indicating the model occasionally misclassifies dramatic or unusual language as sarcasm. The custom Word2Vec embeddings, while effective, lack the semantic richness of pre-trained alternatives. Additionally, the model treats all words equally without attention mechanisms to focus on key sarcastic indicators. Error analysis reveals two primary failure patterns: (1) false positives on headlines with unusual word combinations (e.g., "Prosecutors bizarrely pointed toy gun at grand jury"), and (2) false negatives on subtle sarcasm requiring world knowledge (e.g., "Area man knows all the shortcuts").

Building upon our baseline model achieving 77.50\% accuracy on 4,000 samples, future work will focus on utilizing the complete dataset of 26,709 available samples (6.7× increase in training data). With the significantly larger dataset, we will conduct comprehensive hyperparameter optimization, systematically exploring different configurations of Word2Vec parameters (embedding dimensions, window sizes, negative samples, learning rates), BiLSTM architectures (hidden dimensions, number of layers, dropout rates), and training dynamics (batch sizes, learning rates, schedulers). Through this systematic approach combining full dataset utilization and extensive hyperparameter tuning, we aim to achieve accuracy greater than 90\%, establishing a high-performance sarcasm detection system.

\section{Conclusion}

This work presents a baseline sarcasm detection system achieving 77.50\% accuracy and 89.43\% recall on 4,000 news headlines using custom Word2Vec embeddings and Bidirectional LSTM architecture. The implementation includes Skip-gram with negative sampling built from scratch and a dual-pooling BiLSTM that effectively captures sequential patterns in sarcastic text. Systematic hyperparameter tuning demonstrated that Word2Vec learning rate, training epochs, batch size, and dataset scale synergistically contribute to model performance, with accuracy improving from 66\% (1,000 samples) to 77.50\% (4,000 samples).

The model's exceptional recall (89.43\%) makes it particularly suitable for applications where missing sarcasm is more costly than occasional false positives. However, moderate precision (68.90\%) indicates room for improvement in reducing false alarms on dramatic or unusual language. Future work will utilize the complete 26,709-sample dataset with comprehensive hyperparameter optimization to achieve accuracy greater than 90\%, establishing a high-performance system for practical sarcasm detection applications.
